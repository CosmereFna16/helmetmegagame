BASCINET — TAG DESIGN BRIEF
A note for anyone writing tags, with no knowledge of the codebase assumed.


WHAT THE GAME IS

Bascinet is a month-long asynchronous megagame for 100+ players, half strategy
and half roleplay, set in a cryptic low-fantasy/sci-fi mix. It runs on Discord
plus a companion website. One in-game day maps to one real-world day, split
into two turns — dusk and dawn. Everything happens at the pace of people
checking their phone a few times a day, not at the pace of a table session.

Each player has exactly one living character. On any given turn that character
can do one thing: submit a Move. That's it. A Move is either a Routine (it just
works — you did the sensible thing) or a Gambit (it's risky, so it rolls a d6).
A Move can also be flagged Opposed, meaning someone else is pushing back. A
human GM reads Gambits and decides what happens.

So the whole game is: one action per half-day, adjudicated by a person.


WHAT A TAG IS

A tag is a single fact about a character that a GM should take into account
when reading that character's Move.

That is the entire definition. A tag is not a stat, not a skill tree node, not
a status effect in the videogame sense. It is a line on a character sheet that
answers "what should the GM know before they decide what happens to this
person?"

  Fighting (Skilled)     — this person can actually fight
  Old                    — this person is old
  Horse                  — this person has a horse
  Hungry                 — this person didn't eat and is having a bad day
  Mortus                 — this person tends the dead beneath the city
  Follower (Cook)        — this person has a servant who can cook

Roughly none of this is enforced by code. There is no combat resolution
routine. Nothing checks that you own a bow before you fire one. Tags are a
shared vocabulary between the player and the GM, rendered on a character sheet
and on a hover-tooltip chip in Discord. The two exceptions are noted at the
bottom, and they're small.

This is the single most important thing to internalise, because it changes what
"good" means. A good tag is not one that's mechanically airtight. A good tag is
one that a GM, at 11pm, reading a paragraph of prose from a player they've
never met, can act on without having to look anything up.


THE FIVE RULES

1. ONE TAG, ONE FACT.
   If you can't state what the tag means in one sentence, it's two tags. The
   catalog is a vocabulary, and vocabularies get worse when words get vague.

2. WRITE THE DESCRIPTION FOR THE GM AND THE PLAYER AT ONCE.
   The description text is the actual rules reference — it's what shows in the
   shop, on the character sheet, and in the Discord inspect popup. Say the
   mechanical effect plainly, then let the flavour live in the tag's name.

     Good:  "+1 to the die on all Gambits. Wears off after 2 turns."
     Good:  "When using bows, your fighting skill is two levels higher."
     Bad:   "A quiet confidence settles over you." (a GM cannot act on this)

   You do not need to specify edge cases. A GM is going to adjudicate this
   anyway. Give them a clear default and trust them.

3. A TAG SHOULD OPEN A DOOR, NOT WIN A ROOM.
   The good tags in the existing catalog are the ones that make a *different
   kind of Move* possible. Radio lets you talk to people who aren't in the
   room. Insightful lets you spend a Gambit learning about someone. A horse
   lets you cross a zone without spending your turn. Windlander lets you exist
   somewhere other people can't.

   The weak tags are the ones that are just "+1 to a thing." Those aren't wrong,
   but a hundred of them makes a spreadsheet, not a game. Aim for tags that
   change what a player writes in their Move, not tags that change a number
   inside it.

4. ASYMMETRY IS THE POINT.
   With 100+ players in one world, the interesting thing is that no two people
   can do the same set of things. Tags that only a handful of people have —
   and that other people therefore have to go *find a person* for — generate
   more of the game than tags everyone buys. If a tag is obviously correct for
   every character, it shouldn't cost points; it should just be true.

   Corollary: a tag that makes its owner independent is usually worse than one
   that makes its owner useful to others. Cooking (Skilled) is good design
   because it makes you the person other people need.

5. EVERY TAG SHOULD SUGGEST A WAY TO LOSE IT, OR A REASON IT'S PERMANENT.
   Not literally required — but ask the question. Things that can be taken away
   create plot. Things that can never be taken away define a person. Both are
   fine; drifting into neither is how a catalog gets bland.


THE ECONOMY

A player builds their character by spending points. The default budget is 12,
plus whatever their role grants, minus 3 if they're Cursed (a punishment role
carried over from a dead character).

Costs are SIGNED. A positive cost spends points. A NEGATIVE cost gives points
back — that's how drawbacks work. Old is -2. Frail is -3. Buying a flaw to
afford something better is a legitimate and encouraged build move. The only
legality check is that you can't end up below zero.

Current calibration, so new tags land in the same range:

  1 point   a small, specific perk. Sign Language. Musician. A cheap variant
            of something you already own (Follower (Cook), once you have a
            Follower).
  2 points  a real competence, or an animal. Laborer. Smithing. Forester. A
            horse. Every rung of every tiered skill ladder.
  3 points  strong, scarce, or something that unlocks a location. A Manor.
            Fighting (Archer).
  4+        expected to be the thing the character is *about*. Insightful. The
            revolver.

A tiered ladder costs 2 per rung, so Fighting (Legendary) is a 10-point
commitment out of a 12-point budget. That's deliberate: being the best fighter
in the world means being nothing else. Preserve that shape.

If you're adding a drawback (negative cost), it must be marked as not buyable
after the game starts. Otherwise a player buys it mid-game, banks the points,
and asks a GM to remove it. This is the one rule in the whole system that
exists purely to stop an exploit.


THE TWO RELATIONSHIPS PEOPLE ALWAYS MIX UP

Tags can point at each other in two different ways, and the distinction matters
more than it looks.

  parentTag — "this REPLACES that."
              Fighting (Trained) replaces Fighting (Basic). It's a ladder. You
              stand on one rung.

  requiredTag — "you must already have that, and you KEEP it."
              Fighting (Archer) requires Fighting (Basic), but sits happily
              alongside Fighting (Legendary). It's a branch, not a rung.
              Follower (Cook) requires Follower because it's describing that
              same follower.

Test: does the new tag make the old one pointless? Then it's a parent. Does the
new tag build on the old one, with both still true? Then it's a requirement.


THE RHYTHM YOU'RE DESIGNING INTO

Two things run automatically every turn, and they're worth knowing because they
set the emotional baseline a tag is layered on top of.

HUNGER. At the close of every turn, every character either pays one resource
or goes Hungry. Hungry is -1 to every Gambit for a turn. Resources are the
game's single currency — one resource is roughly one meal — so the floor of the
game is a mild, constant subsistence pressure, and food-related tags are load-
bearing rather than flavour.

MOOD. Happy is +1 to Gambits, Unhappy is -1, and both wear off after two turns.
Neutral is just the absence of both. Players set their own mood and say why.
Modifiers stack: an unhappy, hungry character rolls at -2, which on a d6 is
brutal. That's intentional — a character having a bad week should visibly be
having a bad week.

Both of these are just tags, which is a good illustration of how thin the
machinery is meant to be. If your new mechanic can be expressed as "a tag that
appears, does something a GM can read, and expires," it will fit.


THE CATEGORIES

Six, and they're fixed. Adding one is a code decision, so work inside them.

  Meta      Engine plumbing. Flags, not fiction. Players never buy these.
  General   Who the character is. Background, faith, standing, permanent
            traits. Mostly granted by a role rather than bought.
  Skills    What the character can do. All the tiered ladders live here.
  Status    A temporary condition. Usually granted by game logic, usually
            expires on a timer.
  Items     A specific physical object being carried. Can be traded, can be
            crafted.
  Assets    Standing possessions and people. Property, animals, followers.
            Owned, not carried.

The Items/Assets line is the one that gets fuzzy. A revolver is an Item — it's
in your hand and you could give it away. A house is an Asset — it's yours but
it isn't going anywhere. When in doubt: could you hand it to someone across a
table? Item. Otherwise Asset.

Groups are a purely cosmetic layer on top: they tint a tag's chip a colour so
the Status section of a character sheet reads at a glance (green buffs, grey
debuffs, red health). Optional, and no tag is worse for lacking one.


VISIBILITY

Tags default to private. Another player who inspects your character in Discord
sees only the tags explicitly marked visible.

Mark a tag visible when it would be apparent from *looking at or talking to*
the person for a minute. A visible weapon. The way a trained fighter stands.
Declared nobility. Keep it private when it's interior, secret, or something
they'd have to demonstrate: medical training, a follower they left at home,
a secret allegiance.

This is a stealth-and-suspicion game running in a text channel. Visibility is
the main lever you have on how much people can read each other, so spend it
carefully — a catalog where everything is visible is a catalog with no secrets
in it.


COSTS TO ACQUIRE THINGS IN PLAY

Separate from the point cost — which is only about character creation — a tag
can carry an in-play acquisition cost: turns of time, resources, required
skills, and whether it needs a Gambit (meaning it can fail). Forging the
revolver costs 2 turns, 4 resources, and Smithing. Curing Arthritis costs
3 turns and someone with Medical (Skilled).

One block covers whichever direction is narratively interesting. For a crafted
object that's the cost to make it. For an affliction that's the cost to be rid
of it. It's obvious from the tag which one you mean.

Nothing here is enforced by code either — it's a GM's reference and a player's
planning aid. Fill it in for anything a player could plausibly want to acquire
or shed during the month, and leave it blank otherwise.


WHAT NOT TO DO

  Don't write a tag that requires the GM to remember a rule. If it needs a
  paragraph, it isn't ready.

  Don't write a tag whose whole content is a bonus to a roll. Some exist and
  they're fine, but they're the least interesting thing the system can hold.

  Don't build ladders more than a handful of rungs deep. The existing ones cap
  at five for a reason — nobody has the budget, and each rung has to feel
  different from the one below.

  Don't make a tag that removes a constraint entirely. "You never go hungry"
  exists exactly once, as an engine flag, and it isn't purchasable.

  Don't design around a system that doesn't exist. If your tag needs a trading
  economy or a crafting queue to make sense, it's a proposal, not a tag —
  write it in the notes column instead.

  Don't reuse or rename an existing slug. The slug is the tag's permanent
  identity; changing it makes a second tag rather than editing the first.


THE THREE PLACES CODE ACTUALLY LOOKS AT A TAG

For completeness, since "nothing is enforced" is a slight overstatement:

  1. The point-buy menu reads cost and buyability, so those numbers are real.
  2. Hunger and Mood are tags with expiry timers, granted and swept automatically
     each turn, and they feed a modifier into the Gambit die.
  3. Two tags gate a Discord channel each — Radio and Intercom — and a handful
     of tags gate a page or a check elsewhere in the app.

Everything else is a human reading a sentence and making a call. Write for that
human.


HOW TO ACTUALLY SUBMIT TAGS

Use the spreadsheet. Every column header has a hover note explaining that
field, including all the arcane ones above. One row per tag. Leave a cell blank
to accept its default. Put anything you're unsure about in the notes column —
that column exists precisely so you never have to guess, and it isn't imported.

Don't worry about the file format, the sync command, or anything else on the
development side. The sheet is the whole interface.
